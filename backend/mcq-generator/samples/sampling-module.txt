NSSTA Training Module — Sampling Methodology for Official Statistics
Unit 4: Frames, Designs and Precision

1. The sampling frame

A sampling frame is the operational list from which units are actually selected.
It is not the target population; it is the best available approximation of it, and
the gap between the two is the first source of error in any survey.

Two frame errors matter in practice. Over-coverage occurs when the frame contains
units that do not belong to the target population — establishments that have
closed, households that have dissolved, duplicate entries for the same unit.
Under-coverage occurs when units that do belong to the population are absent from
the frame — newly established units, unregistered enterprises, households in
settlements added since the last listing operation.

Over-coverage inflates fieldwork cost and reduces the achieved sample, but is
detectable in the field: an enumerator reaches the address and records the unit as
ineligible. Under-coverage is more damaging because it is invisible from inside the
survey. No amount of weighting recovers a unit that was never eligible for
selection, and no increase in sample size compensates for it, since the additional
units are drawn from the same defective list. Frame errors are corrected by
updating or supplementing the frame, not by adjusting estimates afterwards.

Non-response is a distinct problem and must not be confused with frame error. A
non-respondent is a unit that was correctly on the frame and correctly selected but
from which data could not be collected. Non-response is addressed through weighting
adjustments or imputation; frame error is not.

2. Simple random sampling as a benchmark

Under simple random sampling without replacement, every unit in the frame has an
equal and known probability of selection, and every combination of units of a given
size is equally likely. It is rarely used directly in large-scale official surveys,
because it requires a complete unit-level frame and produces samples scattered
across the whole country, which is expensive to enumerate.

Its role is as a benchmark. The precision of any complex design is conventionally
expressed relative to the precision simple random sampling would have achieved with
the same number of units.

3. Stratification

Stratification divides the frame into mutually exclusive and exhaustive groups —
by state, by sector, by size class — and draws an independent sample within each.
Because between-stratum variation is removed from the sampling error, stratification
generally improves precision relative to simple random sampling, and it guarantees
that each stratum is represented rather than leaving representation to chance.

Stratification requires that the stratifying variable be known for every unit on
the frame before selection. A variable measured during the survey cannot be used to
stratify it.

4. Cluster sampling and the design effect

Cluster sampling selects groups of units — villages, urban blocks, enumeration
areas — and then enumerates units within the selected groups. It reduces travel and
listing cost substantially, which is why nearly all large household surveys are
clustered.

The cost saving is paid for in precision. Units within a cluster tend to resemble
one another, so each additional household within a selected village contributes
less new information than a household selected independently would. The design
effect quantifies this: it is the ratio of the variance of an estimate under the
actual design to the variance that simple random sampling would have produced with
the same sample size.

A design effect of 2.0 means the clustered design has twice the variance of simple
random sampling at equal sample size. To achieve the precision that n units would
have delivered under simple random sampling, the clustered design requires
approximately n multiplied by the design effect. The design effect is therefore a
sample-size planning input, applied before fieldwork, and not merely a correction
applied to standard errors at the analysis stage. Design effects are estimated from
previous rounds of the same or a similar survey.

5. Rotational panel designs

Surveys that measure change over time — labour force participation, prices,
enterprise activity — commonly use rotational panels. A selected unit remains in
the sample for a fixed number of consecutive rounds and is then replaced, so that
consecutive rounds share part of their sample.

This overlap is deliberate. Estimates from two rounds that share units are
positively correlated, and that correlation reduces the variance of the difference
between them. Since the primary output of such a survey is the change from one
period to the next, overlap is what makes the headline figure precise. A design
that replaces the entire sample every round sets the overlap to zero and produces
noticeably noisier change estimates, even though each individual round is estimated
just as well.

The cost of repeated interviewing is panel conditioning: households interviewed
several times may begin to answer differently, whether through fatigue, learning
what a shorter answer avoids, or genuine changes in reporting behaviour. Rotation
schemes bound this by limiting the number of rounds any unit remains in the sample.
